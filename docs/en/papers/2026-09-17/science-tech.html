Scientific Research Briefing

This subsection features four research advances. Scientists developed a solar-powered desalination system that recovers salt as a solid while extracting lithium; an immune sensor called cGAS may mistake DNA damage for infection and drive rapid aging; a new fossil from Tanzania challenges the early dinosaur timeline; and researchers reconstructed videos from mouse visual cortex activity alone.

Scientists turn seawater into fresh water without harmful brine ScienceDaily 科学

Scientists have developed a solar-powered desalination system that turns seawater into fresh water while removing nearly all leftover salt as a solid instead of producing harmful brine. The self-cleaning technology could also recover valuable minerals such as lithium, potentially turning desalination waste into a useful resource.

Read more → Scientists find an immune “false alarm” that may drive rapid aging ScienceDaily 科学

Scientists have discovered that some severe genetic disorders linked to rapid aging may be driven not just by damaged DNA, but by the body's overreaction to that damage. When broken DNA fragments leak into the wrong part of a cell, an immune sensor called cGAS can mistake them for signs of a viral infection, triggering chronic inflammation and other immune responses.

Read more → A 240-million-year-old fossil just changed the dinosaur timeline ScienceDaily 科学

A newly identified 240-million-year-old animal from Tanzania is forcing scientists to rethink the early dinosaur timeline. Dinodontosaurus isiyavamanda belongs to a group previously known only from South America, linking fossil deposits on the two continents. That connection suggests Tanzania's supposed oldest dinosaur fossils may need to be re-dated.

Read more → Scientists recreated what mice saw from brain activity alone ScienceDaily 科学

Scientists reconstructed videos from the brain activity of mice, producing 10-second clips based only on signals from neurons in the visual cortex. The breakthrough could help reveal how the brain reshapes what we see before it becomes our perception of reality, offering new insight into visual processing.
