Space, Energy & Robotics Briefing

This subsection presents four developments spanning an exotic quantum material, shape-shifting knitted fabrics, living bacterial circuits and new views of Mars’s interior. Together they illustrate how materials science, synthetic biology and planetary geophysics are converging, while pointing toward future computing, soft robotics and deep-space exploration.

Quantum oscillations defy expectations in this exotic material ScienceDaily 技术

Researchers observed unusual electron behaviour in zirconium pentatelluride, a quantum material that can act as an insulator and a conductor. Close to absolute zero, under magnetic fields up to 60 tesla, electrons generated quantum oscillations that persisted even after conventional physics said they should stop, challenging the standard description of such materials.

Read more → Harvard scientists turn knitting into shape-shifting smart fabric ScienceDaily 技术

Harvard researchers turned conventional knitting into a platform for programmable smart fabrics. Using elastic yarns and industrial knitting techniques, they produced thick textiles that naturally bend and lock into multiple stable configurations, switching between shapes much like a light switch. Such fabrics could open up new applications in robotics and wearable technology.

Read more → MIT turns bacteria into living transistors ScienceDaily 技术

MIT researchers engineered bacterial 'transistors' that can be wired together into living circuits capable of calculation and chemical signalling. The team envisions coating plant roots or leaves with these biological computers, where they could detect environmental threats and trigger defences automatically.

Read more → Deep beneath Mars, scientists find a vast hidden magma system ScienceDaily 技术

Seismic measurements have revealed vast, layered magmatic systems deep inside Mars, previously thought to require Earth-like plate tectonics. The findings suggest molten material repeatedly evolved and recycled through the Martian crust over hundreds to thousands of kilometres, overturning long-held assumptions about planetary evolution on relatively small rocky worlds.

Scientific Research Briefing

This subsection features four research highlights: 80,000-year-old projectile points that may rewrite the timeline of advanced weaponry; Earth's magnetic field used as a giant dark-matter sensor; an eight-letter DNA alphabet read successfully by RNA polymerase; and a sea-squirt compound that reverses key signs of ageing in mice. The stories span archaeology, cosmology, molecular biology and ageing research.

80,000-year-old “arrowheads” rewrite human history ScienceDaily 科学

Archaeologists in Uzbekistan have identified tiny projectile points dating back about 80,000 years, possibly functioning like arrowheads. Their size, design and impact damage closely match much younger weapons from a Homo sapiens site in France, hinting that sophisticated projectile technology emerged in Central Asia long before modern humans expanded widely.

Read more → A mysterious signal around Earth could be dark matter ScienceDaily 科学

Scientists used Earth's magnetic field and atmosphere as a planet-sized detector to hunt for some of the lightest proposed dark matter particles. The effort sharply improved limits on ultralight axions and turned up several tantalising dark-photon signals that still await explanation.

Read more → Life uses 4 DNA letters. Scientists just made 8 work ScienceDaily 科学

UC San Diego researchers showed that a central cellular enzyme can accurately read an eight-letter genetic alphabet—doubling the four letters used by life on Earth. Detailed imaging revealed RNA polymerase processes synthetic DNA letters much like natural ones, bringing scientists closer to engineered organisms with expanded genetic codes.

Read more → Stanford scientists discover a seafood that can reverse signs of aging ScienceDaily 科学

In older mice, supplements of plasmalogen sourced from edible sea squirts appeared to reverse signs of ageing: memory and learning improved, brain cell connections strengthened, inflammation decreased, and coats grew thicker and darker. The compounds may promote brain regeneration and protect ageing synapses.
