Scientific Research Briefing

This scientific research subsection features four studies. A large cohort links more REM sleep to lower risks of 83 diseases; a Swedish study associates rare congenital TORCH infections with higher autism and intellectual disability risks; neutron and X-ray imaging reveals fossilized blood vessels in a broken rib from the largest known T. rex; and a comparison of three Clusia species shows some tropical trees use nocturnal CAM photosynthesis to cope with drought.

More REM sleep linked to lower risk of 83 diseases ScienceDaily 科学

A large study tracking more than 95,000 people suggests sleep structure is linked to disease risk. People who spent more time in REM sleep had lower risks of 83 conditions, including dementia, heart failure and Parkinson’s disease, while more deep sleep was associated with lower risks of conditions such as type 2 diabetes.

Read more → A broken rib from the world’s largest T. rex hid a 66-million-year-old secret ScienceDaily 科学

Researchers used neutron and X-ray imaging to look inside a broken rib from Scotty, the largest known T. rex skeleton, without damaging the 66-million-year-old fossil. They found an extraordinary fossilized network of blood vessels formed while the dinosaur was healing, offering rare evidence of dinosaur trauma recovery.

Read more → Rare pregnancy infections may triple the risk of autism ScienceDaily 科学

A major Swedish study of 3.7 million people found that rare TORCH infections passed from a pregnant woman to the fetus are linked to much higher risks of autism and intellectual disability later in life. Children with a congenital TORCH infection were about three times more likely to be diagnosed with autism.

Read more → These tropical trees breathe at night to survive drought ScienceDaily 科学

By comparing three Clusia species, researchers found that some tropical trees have evolved CAM photosynthesis, taking in carbon dioxide at night so they can keep their pores closed during hot days and conserve water. The water-saving strategy did not arise through a single evolutionary change but appeared multiple times in the genus.
