Scientific Research Briefing

This Scientific Research subsection highlights four new findings: ancient DNA pushes plague history back 5,500 years; rising cancer in younger adults is linked to accelerated biological aging; a new Alzheimer’s mechanism is revealed in the 3D genome organization; and a nearly century-old explanation of the inductive effect in chemistry textbooks may be wrong.

Plague was killing entire families thousands of years before the Black Death ScienceDaily 科学

Ancient DNA reveals that plague was already killing hunter-gatherers in Siberia 5,500 years ago, long before cities and flea-driven epidemics. The prehistoric strains may have been especially dangerous because they carried a genetic factor capable of provoking severe immune reactions, researchers say, shedding light on early human-pathogen interactions.

Read more → Cancer is rising in younger adults. Faster biological aging may help explain why ScienceDaily 科学

Cancer rates are rising in adults under 55. New research suggests that people from more recent generations tend to be biologically older than earlier generations at the same chronological age, and accelerated biological aging is linked to increased cancer risk. The findings may help explain the troubling trend and inform prevention strategies.

Read more → Scientists find a new layer of Alzheimer’s hidden in the genome ScienceDaily 科学

Scientists found that the 3D organization of DNA is disrupted in several types of brain cells affected by Alzheimer’s disease, altering how important genes are switched on and off. The discovery reveals a previously underexplored layer of the disease that could open new paths for understanding and eventually treating Alzheimer’s.

Read more → Chemistry textbooks have been getting this wrong for nearly 100 years ScienceDaily 科学

A foundational chemistry concept found in textbooks for almost 100 years may have been taught incorrectly. Researchers say the inductive effect in neutral molecules appears to extend only across one chemical bond, rather than gradually weakening over three or four bonds as traditionally taught. Updating this explanation could improve organic chemistry teaching.
