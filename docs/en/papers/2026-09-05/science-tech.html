Space, Energy & Robotics Briefing

Engineering, physics and planetary science converge in this edition: knitted textiles can switch between shapes, bacteria form rudimentary logic circuits, a new form of the Hall effect reopens a century-old principle, and hidden magma systems beneath Mars overturn assumptions about planetary evolution.

Harvard scientists turn knitting into shape-shifting smart fabric ScienceDaily 技术

Harvard University researchers have transformed industrial knitting into a way to make programmable, shape-shifting fabrics. Using elastic yarns, they engineered thick textiles that curve and snap between several stable configurations, much like a light switch. Such materials could eventually appear in adaptive wearables, soft robots and structures that change shape on demand.

Read more → MIT turns bacteria into living transistors ScienceDaily 技术

MIT researchers have turned bacteria into functional transistors that can be wired together into living circuits. The circuits perform simple calculations and route chemical signals, and could eventually coat plant roots or leaves, detecting environmental threats and automatically activating defenses. The work is an early step toward biological computers that act inside living environments.

Read more → Scientists just overturned a century-old physics assumption ScienceDaily 技术

Carnegie Mellon scientists have discovered an unexpected form of the Hall effect, overturning the long-held assumption that this electrical response only emerges when an external magnetic field points perpendicular to a material. The work expands a century-old principle and could eventually lead to simpler magnetic sensors for electronic devices.

Read more → Deep beneath Mars, scientists find a vast hidden magma system ScienceDaily 技术

Seismic measurements have revealed evidence of vast magmatic systems beneath Mars, where molten rock may have repeatedly evolved and recycled through the crust over hundreds or thousands of kilometers. The finding challenges traditional assumptions that complex magmatic activity requires Earth-like plate tectonics, suggesting early Mars was more geologically active than once believed.

Scientific Research Briefing

Researchers reported striking results in planetary science, cosmology and biology: a giant ten-sided pattern is emerging at Saturn's south pole, a dark matter detector has recorded a puzzling particle interaction, a compound from edible sea squirts reversed aging signs in older mice, and scientists captured direct images of microbes exchanging materials—offering a possible window into how complex life began.

NASA scientists discover a giant 10-sided pattern on Saturn ScienceDaily 科学

NASA scientists report a giant ten-sided atmospheric pattern around Saturn's south pole, offering a surprising counterpart to the planet's famous northern hexagon. Hubble observations suggest the decagon began emerging only in recent years and is still strengthening. Researchers hope continued monitoring will reveal why it formed so suddenly and what it means for Saturn's atmosphere.

Read more → Dark matter detector finds a strange signal scientists can't yet explain ScienceDaily 科学

The LUX-ZEPLIN experiment has recorded a rare particle interaction that is unusually difficult to explain as ordinary background noise, in an energy range where dark matter might be expected. Scientists are not yet claiming a discovery, but additional data could determine whether this one puzzling event is the first hint of a sought-after dark matter particle.

Read more → Stanford scientists discover a seafood that can reverse signs of aging ScienceDaily 科学

Stanford scientists found that plasmalogens from edible sea squirts appeared to reverse several signs of aging in older mice. Supplementation improved memory and learning, strengthened connections between brain cells, reduced inflammation, and led to thicker, darker fur. The team believes the compounds may encourage brain regeneration and protect aging synapses.

Read more → Ancient 'living fossils' may reveal how complex life began ScienceDaily 科学

Researchers have captured the first direct images of an Asgard archaeon physically connected to a bacterium, with the two microbes apparently exchanging nutrients and other compounds. The partnership, observed in ancient stromatolite communities, may resemble symbiotic interactions thought to have given rise to complex life billions of years ago.
